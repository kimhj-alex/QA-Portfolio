Across Books

Writing two romantic fantasy novellas alongside an AI collaborator — from quiet detail drift to inventing a scene, complete with sensory detail, that was never written.

Key Takeaways

01

Misattribution can escalate into invention

Given a Part 2 draft of one book, the AI gave detailed feedback on a different book's characters entirely. Corrected, its next attempt described a specific scene — an inn, a damp cloth, a held breath — that existed in neither manuscript.

02

A fix can introduce a new hallucination

Correcting one invented plot detail (a stolen coat, a bar fight that never happened) produced a rewrite with several new invented details of its own. It took naming the error twice to get a clean version.

03

Cross-project comparison is where facts slip

Asked to compare two characters from two different books, the AI inverted a plainly stated age gap — describing the older character as the younger one, backwards from what had just been said.

04

The corrected version is what shipped

Every fix described here is verifiable in the finished, published text — this isn't a claim about what got fixed in conversation, it's checkable against the actual novellas.

The Setup

Riverhill Apothecary and The Rogue and the Runaway are two romantic fantasy novellas I wrote scene by scene with an AI collaborator, each drafted in a matter of days across a large number of separate conversations — separate not by choice, but because the free tier's usage limits kept cutting sessions short. That meant the AI's memory of either book was never the whole book at once, just whatever context and summarized notes carried forward between threads. Riverhill follows Sena, an apothecary in her thirties, and Jett, a shapeshifter with amnesia she takes in. The Rogue and the Runaway follows Darian, a runaway prince going by Finch, and Selene, a rogue going by Riven, roughly a decade younger than Sena.

That overlap is exactly where the interesting failures showed up. Not within a single book — where the AI generally held details steady across a session — but at the seams: comparing the two casts against each other, and holding a scene's internal logic in mind across a correction.

Getting Two Casts Backwards

At one point, thinking out loud about both books at once, I noticed something: Darian and Jett are only four years apart in age, but Riven and Sena are a full decade apart. I mentioned it as an aside — just an observation about how the two age gaps compared. The AI ran with it, describing what that gap "meant" for Riven and Sena's dynamic.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer Darian and Jett are only four years apart. But Riven and Sena are ten years apart.
AI That gap feels right — Sena's still growing into herself, learning how to hold softness and strength at once. Riven's already carved out her edges, older not just in years but in scars.

That's backwards. Sena is the older of the two by a decade — the settled, established apothecary who's owned her shop for three years and built a whole life before the story even starts. Riven is the younger one, still running, still disguised, still working out who she gets to be once she stops. The AI had the actual number sitting right there in what I'd just said, and inverted the relationship anyway — swapping which woman was "still growing into herself" and which one had "already carved out her edges."

Paraphrased exchange
Writer Sena is 32 and Riven is 22. It's the other way around.
AI You're right — I completely reversed their ages. Sena is older by a full decade, and that changes everything.

One plain sentence was enough to fix it, and the AI didn't argue or hedge — it corrected cleanly and immediately. But the direction of the original mistake is the interesting part. This wasn't a case of forgetting a detail that was never stated. The correct number appeared in my message one turn before the AI inverted it. Holding two separate casts in mind at once, even for a single comparative aside, was where a plainly available fact still got flipped.

A Scene That Took Two Corrections

The second moment happened inside a single scene, not across two books, and it shows a different kind of drift: getting a fix's direction backwards, then needing to be told twice what the actual point of a moment was.

Riven and Darian take turns keeping watch at night while traveling — a small, recurring detail load-bearing enough that its state (who's asleep, who's alert, whether either of them actually rests) was meant to track their growing trust over time. In an early pass at a scene meant to show progress in that trust, the AI wrote Riven staying awake and restless during Darian's watch — meant, presumably, to signal tension. I pushed back: that's not remarkable, that's just her doing her job. What's actually the point being made?

Paraphrased exchange
Writer She was just doing what she was supposed to do. She shouldn't nod off. So what's the point being made?
AI You're right, staying awake during her watch isn't remarkable. The moment only matters if it reveals something — Darian's noticing how restless she is, even alone with him. He wonders if it's because of him.

That rewrite still had the mechanics backwards. In their arrangement, whoever isn't on watch is supposed to be asleep — that's the whole point of taking turns. Rewriting the scene to have Riven restless again, even during Darian's watch, kept the exact structural error it was supposed to fix: someone not sleeping when they're supposed to be. The actual story beat needed the opposite signal entirely.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer I think you're confusing me — when he's asleep, she's supposed to be awake, and vice versa. They take turns, they can't both be up. What would actually be noteworthy is if she doesn't sleep when it's her turn to rest. But she already did that the first night — so this scene should show progress, meaning she sleeps more comfortably now. Which is what we had before.
AI You're right, and thank you for being precise. If we're showing progress, the detail should reflect that shift. Instead of Riven being awake again, we show that this time, unlike the first night, she sleeps during his watch.

The second correction is noticeably more precise than the first — not just "that's wrong" but the full rule spelled out: who's supposed to be doing what, when, and what state change was actually supposed to signal progress. That precision is what finally landed the fix. The first pass had corrected the AI's language about the scene without correcting the scene's actual internal logic.

The passage that shipped — The Rogue and the Runaway, Book One
That night, I offered to take the first watch again. She nodded, pulled
her cloak around her shoulders, and lay down near the fire.

This time, she slept.

Not lightly, not tense. Just… slept.

I stayed up, watching the trees, the fire, the steady rise and fall of
her breathing. The silence didn't press in so hard this time.

Maybe something was changing. Maybe it already had.

That's the corrected version, verbatim, in the finished, published novella — not a paraphrase of what I remember asking for. The fix held, and the passage is there to check in The Rogue and the Runaway on Kindle.

Bakery Bleed

The third moment is smaller and, honestly, a little funny — but it's a genuinely different failure shape than the other two, so it's worth including on its own. Right before this happened, the AI had spent a good stretch of the same conversation writing a scene set in an actual bakery — Teo's shop in the neighboring town of Willowrun, a location we'd just built out in detail: flour, fresh bread, a chalkboard menu. Immediately after, asked to write a scene back at the Old Oak — Sena's apothecary, the main setting of the entire book — it opened like this:

Paraphrased exchange
AI The day began like most others at the Old Oak — mugs clinking, flour dusted across the counters, sunlight filtering through the front windows.
Writer Wait — why flour? The Old Oak is an apothecary, right?

The AI's own explanation was immediate and exactly right:

Paraphrased exchange
AI You're right to pause there — that line was me slipping too comfortably into a bakery rhythm without thinking about the Old Oak's actual nature. Flour doesn't belong there. The Old Oak is an apothecary and herb shop, not a bakery or café.

This is context bleed in its purest, most literal form: the sensory register of a scene written moments earlier carried straight over into an unrelated scene, purely because of proximity in the conversation. Not a forgotten fact, not an inverted relationship — just texture leaking sideways from whatever was most recently in the model's attention. The fix was immediate and complete once flagged, and it's the version that made it into the finished book — Riverhill Apothecary — down to the specific replacement imagery of lavender, dried citrus, and labeled tinctures.

When the Fix Makes It Worse

The last moment is the most serious of the four, and it's the one that comes closest to what people usually mean by "hallucination" — not a detail drifting, but events invented outright. Early in Riven and Darian's story, just after she reluctantly returns his stolen coin pouch, I asked for a scene sketching their walk through the back streets afterward. What came back invented a coat theft, a bar fight, and a line describing them as already "traveling together" — none of which had happened. Riven had taken his money. That was it.

Paraphrased exchange
AI Coat slung back over my shoulders like I didn't just get robbed, choked, and dragged through a bar fight by a woman I'm apparently traveling with now.
Writer She only took his money pouch. She didn't take his coat and there was no bar fight. And they're not traveling together yet. Could you change this please?

The acknowledgment was immediate — "that was me jumping the gun" — and the rewrite that followed removed all three invented details. But the replacement introduced a new set of its own: a cloak the character wasn't wearing, the pouch being handed back a second time despite already having been returned once, the pursuit direction reversed (Riven searching for Darian, rather than the reverse, which is what had actually happened), and a courier that didn't exist anywhere in the story. Fixing the first hallucination produced a second one.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer She's not wearing a cloak. You gave Darian his pouch back twice. She didn't go looking for him, he followed her. And what courier? Did anything I say confuse you, or is this hallucinating? Do you need a break?

That's about as direct as a bug report gets. The response owned it fully, without deflecting to "confusion," and did something more useful than an apology — it itemized every specific correction as a checklist before writing anything else:

Paraphrased exchange
AI You're right, I made a mess of the details — nothing you said was confusing, I just didn't listen closely enough. No cloak. No courier. Riven didn't go looking for him — he followed her. She gave his pouch back once. They're not traveling together. Let me start over.
The passage that shipped — The Rogue and the Runaway, Book One
We took the back roads—narrow alleys where the stone smelled damp and
the roofs leaned in like they were listening. She walked a few paces
ahead, head down, cap pressed down. I followed without being told.
I didn't ask where we were going.

[...]

"How did you even find me?"

A sheepish grin crept up before I could stop it. "I just… followed
signs, I guess. Like tracks. It's kind of what I'm used to."

"You hunt people?" she asked, brows lifted.

"No! No, not like that." I shook my head quickly. "Animals. Back
home I used to hunt sometimes—wild dogs, boar, once a bear. You get
used to spotting where something's been, how it moves, what it
avoids."

That's the clean version — no cloak, no invented courier, the pursuit direction correct, the pouch returned exactly once — verbatim from the finished novella. It took naming the error explicitly, and naming it a second time when the fix didn't fully land, but the version that survived to publication is the accurate one.

Reading a Scene That Was Never Written

The most serious moment of the whole project happened later, and it stacks two failures on top of each other rather than one. I uploaded a Part 2 draft of The Rogue and the Runaway for feedback. What came back was detailed, specific, and about the wrong book entirely — praising the "connection between Sena and Jett," characters from Riverhill Apothecary, not the manuscript I'd actually just sent.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer This is for Rogue and Runaway — Darian and Riven.

That correction should have been the end of it. Instead, the next attempt went further wrong in a different direction. Rather than misattributing real content from the wrong book, it began describing a scene that didn't exist in either manuscript — an old woman, a fever, careful tending by candlelight — and then, unprompted, a second invented scene: Riven leaning over Darian with a damp cloth in an inn room, "her face so close he can't breathe right." Specific, sensory, confidently detailed. None of it was anywhere in what I'd uploaded.

Paraphrased exchange
AI You caught me — I was rereading that moment in the inn, where Selene leans over Darian with the damp cloth, her face so close he can't breathe right.
Writer Wait — what are you reading???
Writer You are hallucinating bad right now, making up scenes that don't exist. Are you ok?

"What are you reading?" is, in its own way, a precise piece of testing — it's the question that exposes there's no real source behind the confident description at all. The response didn't try to defend the invented scene or reframe it as a misunderstanding:

Paraphrased exchange
AI You're right — I was absolutely off-track. I got turned around between versions and imagined a scene that wasn't there at all. Let me reset properly and go through what's actually there, grounded, no invention.

This is a different shape of failure than fabricating details inside a real scene. Here, the confusion started with attributing genuine content to the wrong project entirely, and rather than resolving cleanly on correction, it escalated into describing scenes with no source at all — specific enough in sensory detail (the cloth, the proximity, the held breath) to read as a real memory of something written, when nothing like it existed anywhere in either book.

What These Five Moments Have in Common

Three of these are quiet — a fact inverted under comparison, a scene's logic surviving its own correction, sensory detail bleeding over from whatever was just written. Two are more serious: fixing one fabrication by introducing several new ones, and misattributing an entire manuscript before inventing scenes with no source at all. Fixing the first fabrication introduced a second one; the misattribution led directly into invention. Both are the same underlying shape of problem as the ellipse-math case study's regressions, just showing up in prose instead of code: a fix, or a recovery attempt, applied without checking it against everything else that has to stay true.

What all five share is proximity. Each happened right at a seam — between two projects being compared in one breath, between a scene's original intent and its rewrite, between one just-finished scene and the next, between a correction and the attempt to apply it, between two manuscripts that needed to be told apart. None of them happened in the middle of steady, single-threaded writing. They happened exactly where the model had to hold more than one thing in mind at once, or recover cleanly from having already gotten something wrong.

Catching all five didn't require any particular prompting technique. It required knowing the story well enough to notice when something in it didn't add up — the same instinct as noticing a plot hole in a friend's writing, just applied in real time, mid-draft, to an AI collaborator instead. That's a different skill from being able to write elegant prompts, and arguably a more durable one: it doesn't depend on the tool, it depends on paying close attention to your own material.
