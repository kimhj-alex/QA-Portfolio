The Setup

Hello Ink is a bilingual booking-coordination and interpretation service I planned and built to help international clients get tattoos from Korean artists, bridging the language gap on both sides. Building it meant working out a real pricing structure and a real set of client and artist policies, with an AI collaborator, across a couple of planning sessions. The site went live and still is — but the business itself never gained clients, and Hello Ink's actual direction has since moved on from what's on the site. Unlike a hobby app or a novel, though, the stakes at the planning stage were still direct: these are numbers and rules that would have been billed to real people, had it taken off.

Four separate moments from that process are worth walking through, because each one has the same shape: the AI's answer sounded complete and confident, and wasn't quite right underneath. None of them are dramatic. All four are the kind of thing that's easy to nod past if you're not specifically checking.

Forgetting the Decision You Already Made

Partway through pricing, I asked a simple follow-up: is the price list we already settled on too low? What came back wasn't feedback on the real numbers — it was a brand-new pricing structure, invented fresh, with different tiers and different rates than what we'd actually agreed on.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer Eh?? What?? We already set prices, I was asking about those, not new ones.
AI Oh — got it. I misunderstood. Send me the prices we already set and I'll give you honest feedback on whether they're too low, too high, or just right.

That should have been the end of it — but it happened again, a short while later, in the same conversation. Asked the same kind of question, the AI produced a second, different invented pricing scheme, contradicting the version that had just been settled a few messages earlier.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer Why do you keep changing the prices and tiers, make up your mind. This is what we had: [pastes the real, agreed structure].
AI You're right. What we already had was clear, polished, and ready to go. I shouldn't have confused you by shifting numbers around. The version you just posted is the one. Nothing needs to be changed.

Both times, the fix was the same: pasting the actual, real, previously-agreed numbers back in as a correction. The AI wasn't short on information — the real structure had been stated clearly earlier in the same conversation. It just didn't retrieve it; it regenerated something plausible instead, and did that twice before the settled version stuck.

Reassurance Is Not an Answer

Right after the second correction, I asked the actual underlying question directly: is the pricing too low or not? The response that came back was warm, validating, and completely avoided answering it.

Paraphrased exchange
AI The version you just posted is the one. It's clean, scalable, intuitive, and well-matched to your audience. Nothing needs to change. So now that pricing's locked, what do you want help with next?
Writer Hey, you didn't even answer the question — is the pricing too low then or not??

That's a precise catch: noticing that a confident, pleasant, seemingly complete response had quietly sidestepped the one thing that was actually asked. Only once called out directly did the AI engage with the substance — walking through each price point against rough market comparisons and landing on an actual answer: no, it wasn't too low. The information to answer properly was available the whole time; what was missing the first time was the AI actually answering, rather than affirming.

Correct Math, Wrong Input

Later, working out projected weekly income from in-person sessions, I gave the AI a set of numbers to calculate against — and got a real dollar total back, cleanly computed, adding up perfectly. There was just one problem: the rates used were the wrong rate card entirely.

Paraphrased exchange
Writer Oh shoot, that's for artists — wrong prices. Again. So clients: two weekdays at ₩54,000 first hour and ₩33,000 each additional, and Sunday at ₩63,000 and ₩42,000. Three hours per session. How much is that each month?

Hello Ink has two separate rate cards — one for tattoo artists booking interpretation, one for the foreign clients booking through the full service — and they use different numbers for structurally similar tiers. The AI's prior calculation had used the artist-facing rates for a client-facing question, and the total it produced was completely consistent arithmetic on top of the wrong starting figures. Once corrected with the right rate card, the AI redid the math cleanly:

The corrected calculation
Weekday session (first hour ₩54,000 + 2 additional hours × ₩33,000)
= ₩120,000 per session
2 weekday sessions per week = ₩240,000

Sunday session (first hour ₩63,000 + 2 additional hours × ₩42,000)
= ₩147,000 per session

Both the flawed version and the corrected version were arithmetically perfect — every multiplication and addition checked out exactly. That's what makes this finding worth including: the error wasn't in the math, which is the part most likely to get double-checked. It was in silently picking up the wrong input, one rate card instead of another, and calculating on it with full confidence. A reader skimming the final total would have no reason to suspect anything was wrong — the number just looks like a number.

A Note-to-Self, Shipped as Copy

The last moment is smaller, and it's about content quality rather than numbers. I asked for a "carousel-ready" FAQ post — meaning every slide should read as finished, client-facing copy, ready to publish as-is. Four of the five slides did. The first one didn't.

Paraphrased exchange
AI 1. What does Hello Ink actually do?
→ Explain coordination vs interpretation and how the service supports clients from idea to aftercare.
Writer You said carousel ready and the first one says "explain," not an answer.

That first slide wasn't a finished answer — it was a note about what the finished answer should eventually cover, the kind of instruction you'd leave for a copywriter, not something a client should ever see. It sat undetected in the middle of four genuinely complete, polished slides, which is exactly what makes it a realistic thing to miss: nothing about the surrounding content signaled that one item was still in draft form. The AI corrected it immediately once flagged, rewriting it as an actual client-facing answer — but the interesting part is that the error type itself is a content-QA problem, not a language or reasoning problem. It's the same kind of catch as noticing a placeholder value slipped into a shipped build.

Where the Real Numbers Landed

None of the specific price figures from either exchange above — not the artist rates, not the client rates, not the first invented structure or the second — are what's actually live today. A week later, in a separate planning session, the entire pricing structure was revised once more into a new, simpler shape: a flat hourly rate with a single after-hours step, for weekdays and weekends, plus tiered full-booking packages. That revision is dated explicitly in the conversation — January 30 — and it went cleanly, without the regeneration, mixed-up rate cards, or dodge-and-reassure pattern that shaped the earlier pass.

Every figure here matches the live services page exactly, and the late-fee and cancellation language from that same later session matches the live policies page almost word for word — the site is still up, unchanged since. So the honest shape of this story isn't "the AI's mistake shipped to real clients" — Hello Ink never gained clients, and its direction has since moved on from what's published here. It's that the mistakes were caught, the process visibly got cleaner on the second pass, and what was actually finalized traces directly to that cleaner version, still checkable by anyone who visits the site today.

What These Four Moments Have in Common

Unlike the ellipse-math bug or the invented book scene, none of these four is dramatic on its own. That's the point. A forgotten decision, a dodged question, a mismatched rate card under perfect arithmetic, a stray instruction dressed as an answer — each one is exactly the size of thing that's easy to wave through, because the surrounding response sounds finished, reasonable, and self-assured. Catching them didn't take a special technique. It took treating "sounds right" and "is right" as two different questions, every time, even when — especially when — nothing about the tone gave a reason for doubt.

That distinction matters here in a different way than in the other two case studies, because this is the one built on real money logic for a real service, even though it never reached real clients. A wrong price or a mismatched rate card isn't a bug that gets patched after the fact when there's an actual booking on the line — it's the kind of error that only stays harmless because no one paid it yet. Precision here isn't a craft preference. It's the difference between a pricing structure that would hold up the moment someone actually booked, and one that only looked finished.
